We thank all three reviewers for a careful and constructive reading. The revision changes the
framing of the contribution substantially, adds five tables, a section on reproducing the
results, several new paragraphs and a corrected precision analysis, and fixes several
statements that did not survive our own re-check of the underlying data. We are particularly
grateful to Reviewer~3 for pointing out that the plugin was already announced in the original
publication: acting on that comment led us to rewrite the contribution claim from scratch,
which we believe makes the paper both more accurate and easier to assess.

Two corrections we made on our own initiative deserve to be stated up front, since they change
numbers that appeared in the submitted version:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The claim of near-ideal speedup ``from $N \gtrsim 44$'' was wrong.} While
computing the parallel-efficiency table requested by Reviewer~1, we found that at $N=44$ the
measured efficiency is $43\%$, not $\approx\!100\%$. The correct statement --- now used
throughout --- is that the two samplers cross between $N=40$ and $N=42$, efficiency exceeds
$90\%$ from $N=48$, and the ideal $8\times$ speedup is attained from $N=52$ onwards.
\item \textbf{The precision paragraph quoted a range not covered by our data.} The submitted
text reported deviations and Hamming distances ``at $N=56$, $58$ and $60$''; the verification
tables in fact cover $N=40,\dots,54$ (single-GPU) and $N=60$ (distributed). The paragraph has
been rewritten around the instances that are actually verified --- and, with the CPU heuristic
described under R2.1, the verification now covers all twenty stored runs, $N=38,\dots,60$, so
the claim ends up broader than the one withdrawn.
\end{enumerate}

A \texttt{latexdiff} against the submitted version is provided.

\section*{Reviewer 1}

\point{R1.1}{The paper measures up to 8 H100 GPUs, but claims about scaling to much larger
allocations are back-of-the-envelope projections. The authors also note that controller merge
bandwidth, Ray overhead, and kernel launch latency may limit scaling.}

\textbf{We agree, and we have reduced both the size and the prominence of the projection.}
Three changes:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The projection target is now $2^{14}=16{,}384$ GPUs rather than $2^{16}$. We checked our
original phrasing and it was simply wrong: $2^{16}=65{,}536$ accelerators exceeds the
accelerator count of any system in operation, so calling it ``an allocation within reach of
leadership-class systems'' was not defensible. The revised paragraph gives $N=60$ in
$\approx\!2.2$~min and $N=64$ in $\approx\!35$~min at $2^{14}$ GPUs, with a one-month budget
reaching $N \lesssim 74$. $2^{16}$ now appears only as an explicitly hypothetical bound, with
the observation that quadrupling the resources buys two variables.
\item The projection is confined to a single clearly labelled paragraph (\emph{Scaling outlook
on larger allocations}) and no longer appears in the abstract or the conclusions.
\item It is now \emph{preceded} by a new paragraph, \emph{Controller cost and the limits of the
decomposition}, which states which component would actually break first and why (see R2.2),
and it closes by pointing back to that caveat.
\end{itemize}

The projection is also no longer supported by argument alone. Table~5 of the revision reports
the controller cost up to $2^{16}$ subproblems: $7.4$~s merging directly and $3.8$~s merging
hierarchically. That measurement runs on one machine and so excludes network transfer, which we
state in the caption and in the text --- it bounds the local component from below rather than
estimating the total --- but it is enough to establish that the merge \emph{arithmetic}
($2.1$~s of the $7.4$~s) is not what would limit the projection.
Nor is the linear-scaling assumption an assumption any more within our envelope: Table~4
measures every GPU count and node layout at fixed problem size, giving $2.00\times$ on two
GPUs, $3.99\times$ on four and $7.86\times$ on eight, i.e.\ $98.2\%$ parallel efficiency, with
weak scaling flat to within $0.2\%$. We measured the whole series twice, on separate days, and
quote the reproducibility in the table caption.

What remains extrapolated is behavior beyond eight devices and beyond one node boundary. Item
(v) of \emph{Limitations and intended scope} now states exactly that.

\point{R1.2}{The paper positions the plugin as a ground-truth oracle and briefly checks results
against SBM, but it does not provide a broad benchmark against multiple exact or heuristic
solvers across different instance families.}
% PENDING SUBMISSION: replace the E8 outcomes/timings below from the H100 production JSON.

\textbf{Agreed. We have added a second, algorithmically distinct heuristic and evaluated both
heuristics on the same certified instances from four families.}

The plugin studied here performs complete enumeration. For this implementation, every run
evaluates all $2^{N}$ assignments, so its own runtime is essentially independent of coefficient
family apart from negligible best-state bookkeeping. This statement is specific to the plugin;
we have removed the earlier generalization to exact solvers as a class. Branch-and-bound and
structure-aware methods may prune the search or exploit instance properties, and their
performance can depend strongly on the family.

At $N=44$ we generated five instances from each of four families: couplings uniform on
$[-1,1]$, bimodal $J_{ij}\in\{-1,+1\}$, Gaussian, and sparse at $20\%$ density. On every
instance we ran two different heuristic mechanisms against the exhaustive certificate. The dSB
solver uses $4096$ replicas, $3000$ integration steps, seed 42, \texttt{float64} dynamics and its
automatically tuned time step. The simulated-annealing solver uses
\texttt{dwave.samplers.SimulatedAnnealingSampler}, $4096$ reads, $3000$ sweeps, a geometric
inverse-temperature schedule, randomized update order and deterministic seeds
$420000,\dots,420019$. These are documented method-specific budgets; we do not equate a dSB
integration step with an SA sweep.

Both heuristics reached a certified optimum on \textbf{all twenty instances}. The dSB state was
identical to the stored certified state on seventeen instances and the SA state on sixteen. All
different states occur in the bimodal family and have the same integer energy, so they are
degenerate ground states rather than optimization failures. Independent \texttt{float64}
evaluation placed each selected state's energy within $3.2\times10^{-13}$ of the certificate. Both
heuristics are rerun by the same E8 driver on the same host. The new Table~6 reports optimum
hits and median wall time by family; the complete states, seeds, gaps,
timings and instance hashes are in
\texttt{benchmarks/results/multi\_solver/N44.json}, produced by
\texttt{benchmarks/exp\_multi\_solver.py}.

For implementation correctness we retain the comparison with \texttt{dimod.ExactSolver} at
CPU-enumerable sizes and independent \texttt{float64} recomputation at benchmark scale. These
are correctness checks, not claims about comparative performance against every exact method.

\point{R1.3}{The paper claims near-ideal speedup, but does not explicitly calculate or report
parallel efficiency.}

\textbf{Agreed and added --- and, as noted above, doing so revealed an error in the submitted
version.}

The revision contains a new table (Table~3) and a new paragraph (\emph{Strong scaling and
parallel efficiency}). Both samplers were run on identical instances, so the two curves of
Fig.~1 pair size by size into a strong-scaling measurement. Computed from the raw result files,
the speedup $S=t_1/t_8$ and the efficiency $E=S/8$ are:

This shows that our original statement --- full $\approx\!8\times$ speedup ``from
$N \gtrsim 44$'' --- was incorrect, and we have corrected it in the abstract, in
\emph{Architecture}, in \emph{Performance} and in the caption of Fig.~1. Below the crossover
the decomposition is dominated by fixed overheads; at $N=40$ the eight-way split is in fact
$2.1\times$ \emph{slower} than a single GPU, which we now state explicitly as the reason the
single-GPU sampler remains the right choice whenever one device suffices.

We report the value at $N=54$ as measured ($E=100.1\%$) together with a one-sentence
explanation that it lies within the run-to-run variability of these single-run timings and
should be read as ideal scaling rather than as superlinear speedup, rather than rounding it

efficiency: being the first measurement of its sweep, $t_1$ there includes the one-off creation
of the CUDA context, and drops from $2.74$~s to $2.11$~s when re-measured after a warm-up.

\textbf{The reviewer is right. The data now travels with the article rather than in a separate
repository.} The
instances, the raw per-run results and the verification tables are included in the article

\begin{center}
\small

\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The benchmark and plotting scripts sit alongside them in \texttt{benchmarks/} and read from
these paths, so Fig.~1 and Table~3 can be regenerated from a single checkout with no external
repository involved. Section~3 of the revision is a guide to that directory, and the manuscript
now points at this one location consistently.

Regarding the SBM parameters specifically, the answer is now structural rather than a corrected
hyperlink. The comparison heuristic is a discrete simulated-bifurcation solver that we
\textbf{distribute with the article} as \texttt{benchmarks/sbm.py}, driven by
\texttt{benchmarks/verify\_sbm.py}. Verification against a simulated-bifurcation solver was
part of the submitted manuscript; what this revision adds is that the solver performing it now
travels with the data, so there is no external script to point at and nothing to go stale.

Its configuration is also stated inline in the paragraph \emph{Verification against a
simulated-bifurcation solver}: $2^{12}=4096$ parallel replicas, $3{,}000$ integration steps, an
automatically tuned time step, and the lowest-energy replica reported. A parameter list in the
paper, backed by the code that implements it, is more durable than a pointer to a script.

The implementation is deliberately structured so that the comparison is cheap to repeat: all
replicas are integrated simultaneously, so one step is a single dense
$(N\!\times\!N)(N\!\times\!4096)$ product and the whole run costs
$\mathcal{O}(N^{2}R\,\times\,\mathrm{steps})$ --- independent of the exponential cost of
\emph{certifying} the optimum, and a matter of seconds even at $N=60$ on a CPU. A reader with
no GPU can therefore reproduce the entire verification from this repository alone.

Doing so also let us widen the claim. The heuristic is now compared against \textbf{every}
brute-force result we hold --- all twenty runs, $N=38,\dots,60$, rather than the nine of the
submitted version --- and on each of them it returns a configuration identical to the certified
ground state, with energies agreeing to $6\times10^{-14}$. The verification and precision
paragraphs have been widened accordingly.

\point{R2.2}{While the code achieves near ideal strong scaling for $N \ge 44$, corresponding
weak scaling tests are absent \dots\ This should help quantify the slowdown associated with the
controller merge step \dots\ which should ideally scale as the logarithm of processor count
\dots\ running this algorithm on $2^{16}$ processors could result in an algorithm that is
limited by this communication step.}

\textbf{We added the requested weak-scaling study and measured the local controller
components, while making the remaining network and large-allocation limitations explicit.}

First, the weak-scaling study, in the design the reviewer proposed. Table~4 of the revision
reports $N=50,51,52,53$ at $1$, $2$, $4$ and $8$ GPUs, with $k$ growing alongside the device
count so that every GPU always enumerates $2^{50}$ configurations. The wall-clock time stays
between $2112.5$~s and $2116.9$~s across the whole series: flat to within $0.2\%$, with the
merge contributing $0.007$~s throughout. The same table reports strong scaling on the same
layouts, and both halves were measured twice on separate days.

Second, the controller. The measurement does \emph{not} require a large GPU allocation, because
the number of subproblems is set by \texttt{num\_fixed\_vars} and is independent of the number
of devices: Ray simply queues $2^{k}$ tasks onto whatever is available, so the controller can be
driven on a CPU with the partial results a worker would have returned. Table~5 reports that
sweep up to $2^{16}$ subproblems, and three things follow.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The direct merge is linear rather than logarithmic}, as the reviewer anticipated
might become problematic for a controller that collects every partial result: we fit
$O(P^{0.97})$ over four decades.
\item \textbf{The merge arithmetic is not what would limit it.} At $2^{16}=65{,}536$
subproblems the concatenation accounts for $2.1$~s of the $7.4$~s direct-merge total. We want to
be explicit about the limits of this measurement, though, because it bears directly on the
reviewer's question: it runs on a single machine, so every partial result is fetched from the
local object store and no network transfer is involved at all. It is a lower bound on the
controller cost at a given $P$, covering the arithmetic and the local scheduling, and the
manuscript now says so in both the text and the table caption.
\item \textbf{The dominant term is not the merge at all}, but Ray's per-task scheduling:
even with no network involved, dispatching and collecting $2^{16}$ \emph{empty} tasks costs
$5.4$~s, more than the entire hierarchical merge. This is a scheduling floor, not the total
cost of a payload-bearing task: serialization, result delivery and materialization are additional
terms. We have therefore identified task grouping as a concrete future-work direction without
claiming that scheduling alone sets the large-allocation ceiling.
\end{enumerate}

The network term remains separate. Ray may return a small value inline with the task-completion
reply, which avoids a second object-store pull but does not avoid transmission: the result bytes
still cross the network in that reply. Table~5 uses empty tasks for its scheduling floor and all
of its merge inputs are local, so we retain its lower-bound interpretation and do not infer a
network cost from it.

To probe the aggregate effect omitted from Table~5 on the available two-node cluster, the
reproduction package now includes \texttt{benchmarks/exp\_boundary\_cost.py}. Its timer starts
before submission and ends only after \texttt{ray.get} has materialized every result. Matched
tasks receive the same request and construct the same payload, but only one returns it; local,
remote and mixed placements are measured and every repetition is retained. The
payload-minus-discard contrast measures the incremental result-return path within each
placement, while the remote-minus-local difference between those contrasts is our observable
for the node-boundary effect. The measurement follows Ray's ordinary task-return path rather
than forcing a particular storage mechanism, and therefore does not separately identify
serialization, transport, scheduling or materialization. We do not fold this two-node
diagnostic into Table~5 or extrapolate it to thousands of raylets; head-node and fabric
saturation remain outside the available hardware envelope.

This uncertainty strengthens the architectural case for hierarchical reduction: the tree leaves
the controller receiving one object instead of $P$, reducing central ingress and memory pressure
without relying on a favorable two-node timing.

We also implemented the hierarchical reduction. It is not uniformly better: it overtakes the
direct merge only from $P=64$ upwards, because below that
the concatenation is cheap enough that performing it in remote tasks merely adds rounds of
scheduling. Both strategies ship, and the released sampler selects between them on that
measured threshold.

We have also defined what the reported times measure, which the submitted version did not. For
the single-GPU sampler the timer brackets the CUDA search itself, excluding host-side assembly
of the QUBO matrix and decoding of the returned configurations; for the distributed sampler it
brackets subproblem dispatch and completion of every subproblem search, excluding the final
merge, which is now reported separately in the sampler's own metadata. Both exclusions are
polynomial in $N$ and negligible at the reported sizes, but they matter for the extrapolations,
and the reader is entitled to know.

Finally, the trade-off in $k$ is now explicit: at fixed $N$, incrementing $k$ halves the work
per subproblem while doubling the number of results to combine, so a break-even point in $k$
necessarily exists. The measurement locates it rather than leaving it as a conjecture.

\point{R2.5}{The manuscript could also benefit from providing speedup with float32 numbers for
\texttt{num\_states = 1} by conducting the simulation at the default float64 precision. This
will help users \dots\ quantify the expected slowdown.}

\textbf{Measured, and the answer is not what we expected.} Repeating the ground-state search at
$N=40,\dots,46$ in both precisions on one H100 gives a \texttt{float64} penalty of only
$2.9$--$3.3\%$ (ratios $1.03$, $1.03$, $1.03$ at $N=42,44,46$), with both precisions returning the
same configuration at every size. The enumeration is bound by memory traffic and integer
bookkeeping rather than by floating-point throughput, so on this hardware single precision buys
working-set size rather than speed. We have corrected the manuscript accordingly: the
\emph{Architecture} paragraph previously justified \texttt{float32} as maximizing throughput,
which is not true here.

Two defects had to be fixed before the experiment was even possible, and both changed what a
user gets. The SPIN$\,\to\,$BINARY recursion in the distributed sampler dropped the requested
\texttt{dtype}, so SPIN inputs always ran in single precision; and the command line's
\texttt{--dtype} never worked at all, because NumPy resolves both \texttt{"float"} and
\texttt{"double"} to \texttt{float64} --- so from the command line \emph{both} choices ran in
double precision and the stabilized single-precision path was unreachable.

The more useful finding for the reviewer's purpose is that double precision is \textbf{not} a
substitute for the stabilization, since the compensated updates and the re-anchoring are
\texttt{float32} only. Up to $N=44$ the \texttt{float64} accumulator stays within $10^{-13}$ of
the from-scratch recomputation, but at $N=46$ it drifts to $2.7\times10^{-5}$ --- marginally
\emph{worse} than the $2.3\times10^{-5}$ of the \emph{stabilized} single-precision run at the
same size. Both still return the true optimum, which we confirmed independently. Our guidance
is therefore to use the default \texttt{float32} path and to recompute the energy of the
returned configuration in \texttt{float64} on the host when a certified numerical value is
needed; extending the stabilization to the double-precision path is now listed as future work.

\section*{Reviewer 3}

\point{R3.1}{$N$ is never defined, even though it plays a central role in interpreting the
results. It would be helpful to briefly reintroduce Omnisolver and its key parameters in the
introduction.}

\textbf{Both points addressed.} $N$ is now defined at its first substantive use in the
abstract, again in the second paragraph of Section~1, and once more in the caption of Fig.~1,
as the number of binary variables of an instance (equivalently, the number of Ising spins), so
that exhaustive search enumerates all $2^{N}$ configurations.

The same paragraph introduces the sampler parameters that previously appeared in the code
listing without explanation: \texttt{num\_states}, \texttt{num\_fixed\_vars}$\,=k$,
\texttt{suffix\_size} (with the meaning that $2^{\texttt{suffix\_size}}$ configurations are
resident in the GPU working set at a time and the search sweeps
$2^{N-\texttt{suffix\_size}}$ such chunks), and the launch parameters \texttt{grid\_size},
\texttt{block\_size}, \texttt{num\_steps\_per\_kernel} and
\texttt{partial\_diff\_buffer\_depth}. Section~1 also opens with a short reminder of how
Omnisolver plugins are registered and what the framework provides.

\point{R3.2}{The omnisolver-bruteforce plugin is already mentioned in the original paper [1].
Therefore, statements such as ``this update introduces omnisolver-bruteforce'' \dots\ are
misleading. The paper should clearly state what is actually introduced by the software update
described here.}

\textbf{The reviewer is correct, and on checking we found the problem to be broader than
stated.} The original publication not only lists the plugin and notes that it can be
GPU-accelerated; it also reports a benchmark of the GPU sampler on $1$, $2$, $4$ and $8$ NVIDIA
A100 GPUs, and describes the plugin as usable up to about $50$ variables. Our submitted
manuscript therefore claimed \emph{two} things as new that were not: ``first-class Omnisolver
plugin'' and ``distributed multi-GPU execution''. We have rewritten the contribution claim
accordingly:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item The \textbf{title} changed to ``\dots\ --- a numerically stabilized, distributed GPU
exhaustive-search plugin'', which also removes the double colon.
\item The \textbf{abstract} now says the plugin, ``together with a preliminary multi-GPU
benchmark, was already announced in the original publication'' before listing what this update
adds.
\item A \textbf{new paragraph}, \emph{Relation to the original publication and to the
predecessor solver}, states the provenance explicitly --- the plugin entry and the
1/2/4/8-A100 benchmark in ref.~[1], and the single-GPU CUDA solver of ref.~[2] underneath it
--- and concludes that what the update contributes is not the existence of a GPU-accelerated or
multi-GPU exhaustive search, but its maturation into a dependable certification backend.
\item The \textbf{contribution list} was rewritten around what is verifiable in the released
code: (i) a released, versioned, API-stable distributed sampler in place of the unreleased code
path behind the earlier benchmark; (ii) numerical stabilization of the \texttt{float32}
ground-state path; (iii) 64-bit-safe enumeration bookkeeping; (iv) certification reach extended
from $N\approx50$ to $N=60$, i.e.\ three orders of magnitude in enumerated configurations, with
an explicit strong-scaling analysis; (v) end-to-end verification of the returned optima.
\item The \textbf{conclusions} open with the same framing rather than with ``promotes \dots\ to
a first-class Omnisolver plugin''.
\end{itemize}

\point{R3.3}{To properly assess the scalability of the distributed sampler, experiments should
include more than two nodes \dots\ it would be interesting to investigate whether the
controller becomes a bottleneck as the number of nodes increases. For these reasons, the
scaling outlook also appears somewhat optimistic.}

\textbf{We agree on all three counts, and two of them we can now answer with measurements
rather than with argument.}

\emph{Does the controller become a bottleneck?} Not through its arithmetic. Table~5 measures
the local cost up to $2^{16}$ subproblems --- $7.4$~s collecting directly, $3.8$~s reducing
hierarchically, against Ray's $5.4$~s task-scheduling floor for the same number of tasks --- so
scheduling already exceeds merging before any network is involved. We are careful not to claim
more than that: the measurement runs on one machine and excludes both the transfer of $P$
partial results into one node and any contention at the head. Those are the terms we expect to
bind. As described under R2.2, inline Ray returns still carry their bytes in the task-completion
reply, so we do not treat them as transfer-free. The revised manuscript keeps Table~5 as a local
lower bound and leaves many-node transfer and contention explicitly unmeasured.

\emph{Does the node layout matter?} Not measurably. Holding the GPU count fixed and adding a
node boundary --- $2\times1$ against $1\times2$, and $2\times2$ against $1\times4$ --- changes
the wall-clock time by $-0.03\%$ and $-0.12\%$ respectively; the two-node configuration is
slightly faster in these runs, but the differences are below the experiment's resolution.

We should report how we arrived at that, because our first attempt said otherwise. A first pass
of the series gave $+1.47\%$ and $+1.12\%$, which we were prepared to present as a small but
real boundary cost. Repeating the entire series on a different day showed that single-node
configurations reproduce to within $0.02\%$ while two-node ones move by up to $1.5\%$ --- and
that the first pass had simply caught the two-node points on a slower day. The correct statement
is therefore that a single node boundary costs nothing we can resolve, with an upper bound of
about $1.5\%$ set by the reproducibility of two-node timings rather than by any systematic
effect. This is what the absence of inter-worker communication predicts, and it also locates the
second host as the source of what run-to-run variation there is.

\emph{More than two nodes} is the one question our hardware cannot settle: two nodes with four
H100s each, connected by standard Ethernet, is the entire cluster available to us. Rather than
leave that implicit, item~(v) of \emph{Limitations and intended scope} states it, and extending
the study past two node boundaries is now item~(ii) of the conclusions rather than a silence:
one boundary costs nothing we can resolve, but an effect below our $1.5\%$ bound could still
compound over many of them.

The scaling outlook itself has been made less optimistic in the concrete sense described under
R1.1: $2^{14}$ instead of $2^{16}$ GPUs, and the observation that $2^{16}$ exceeds the
accelerator count of any existing system.

\point{R3.4}{QUBO instances are not evaluated in the paper, even though they are an important
target use case for the framework.}

\textbf{The measured path is in fact the QUBO path, and it is now demonstrated rather than
argued.} Both samplers convert a SPIN model to its BINARY equivalent, solve it as a QUBO on the
GPU, and convert the result back, so the timings in Fig.~1 are QUBO timings; the only
Ising-specific cost is an $O(N^{2})$ host-side transformation performed outside the measured
region. Loading the same coefficient files once with each variable type and solving both, the
ratio of BINARY to SPIN wall-clock time is $0.998$--$1.000$ over $N=42,\dots,50$. The energies
differ, as they must --- the same coefficients define different objectives under the two
variable types --- but the runtimes do not.

Which versions of the CUDA Toolkit are supported?}
